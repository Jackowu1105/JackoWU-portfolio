## Problem Definition

A food-ordering system for residential communities is a dual-sided design problem: the resident's experience must be effortless, and the restaurant owner's experience must be simple enough for a non-tech-savvy user to operate during a kitchen rush.

### User Pain Points

- **Residents:** calling restaurants was inconvenient during busy hours, with no way to browse menus or track order status — leading to uncertainty about pickup times
- **Restaurants:** phone orders interrupted kitchen workflow, were prone to errors, and missed calls during peak hours meant lost revenue. There was no standardised way to notify users when orders were ready

### Design Goals

- **Speed and transparency for users** — browse menus quickly, know exactly when to pick up
- **Simplicity for merchants** — a straightforward interface to receive and update orders without technical complexity
- **Real-time communication** — both sides need instant notifications to reduce friction

## Design Process

### Research & Discovery

Due to a tight timeline, research focused on **stakeholder interviews** to understand business goals and technical constraints, **competitive analysis** of major food-delivery apps (Uber Eats, Deliveroo, foodpanda) to identify best practices, and **proto-personas** based on stakeholder insights (validated post-launch).

### Information Architecture

Mapped dual-sided user flows:

**User app:** Browse Restaurants → View Menu → Customise Order → Place Order → Receive Notification → Pick Up

**Merchant app:** Receive Order → Update Status (Preparing → Ready) → Notify User

### Wireframes & Iteration

I created low-fidelity wireframes for both apps, focusing on simplicity (minimising steps to complete core tasks), clarity (clear status indicators for both users and merchants), and consistency (shared design patterns across both apps). After internal review with the PM and developers, I refined the menu browsing (added category filters for faster navigation), order status (introduced a progress bar instead of a simple "confirmed" message), and merchant dashboard (simplified order cards to show only essential info).

### Usability Testing

Due to timeline constraints, formal usability testing was conducted after the initial launch with a small group of beta users. Most users completed checkout without assistance, appreciated the progress bar but wanted more detailed ETAs, and merchants requested bulk order management for peak hours.

## Visual Design

### Design System

**Color:** A clean white base (`#FFFFFF`) with a vivid teal primary (`#02B3B9`) for actions and brand accent, paired with warm orange (`#EBAD7E` / `#F8B64B`) for food-related highlights and success states. The teal keeps the app feeling fresh and modern, while warm tones carry the food/friendliness cue.

## Outcomes & Feedback

Several restaurants were onboarded within the first couple of months. Ordering moved from a phone call to a quick in-app flow, and in usability testing most users completed orders independently. The 3-stage progress indicator proved critical for user trust — residents knew exactly when to leave their apartment.

## Reflections

This project taught me how to design for two distinct user groups while maintaining a cohesive experience. Balancing simplicity for consumers with sufficient control for merchants required prioritising core features first and planning for power-user features later. Real-time communication proved critical for user trust — the progress bar was a small UX decision that significantly reduced user anxiety.
