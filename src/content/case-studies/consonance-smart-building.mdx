Smart Building Plus spans four touchpoints, so I opened with a Double Diamond aimed at one question: what does a resident’s day actually look like, and where do the building’s physical rituals create friction? Research mapped those rituals, definition picked the moments worth fixing, development shaped one ecosystem across every touchpoint, and delivery tested it with the residents who live it.

## Project Scope

Smart Building Plus is one ecosystem across four touchpoints, each designed for a different moment in a resident's day:

1. **The Resident App** — the hub every resident carries in their pocket
2. **Access Control** — one QR-based system replacing keys and cards across the building
3. **Kiosk Experience** — guest registration and clubhouse booking, standing up at the lobby
4. **Pool Locker** — securing belongings poolside without carrying keys

Each touchpoint gets its own section below.

## Research & Discovery

I conducted **stakeholder interviews** with property managers, **user interviews** with residents across a range of ages, and **competitor analysis** of smart building apps from similar luxury developments in Hong Kong.

### Key Findings

- Most residents wanted opening the app to be faster than fumbling for a physical key
- Many had security concerns about unauthorised access if they lost their phone
- Residents strongly preferred a single unified app over installing several separate facility apps
- Coming home was a recurring pain point — unlocking the door, calling the lift, and adjusting the apartment (lights, air-con) were three disconnected actions residents did every day
- A single QR system should cover every access point, so residents never carry a pocketful of keys and cards

### User Personas

**Primary: Busy Professionals (30–45)** — work long hours, frequently travel, value speed and efficiency above all. Uses the app primarily during morning and evening rush hours. *"I need to get in the building quickly when I'm carrying groceries."*

**Secondary: Elderly Residents (60+)** — less tech-savvy, needs intuitive interfaces, concerned about security and privacy. Requires larger tap targets and clear labels. *"I don't want too many complicated features."*

## The Resident App

### Information Architecture

I organised the app's features into three categories based on frequency of use: **Access Control** (the bulk of daily usage — lobby, clubhouse, mailbox, locker), **Reservations** (less frequent — clubhouse, facilities), and **Visitor Management** (occasional — kiosk registration).

### Key Design Decisions

**QR code for access** — Faster than Bluetooth pairing, works offline, and familiar for Hong Kong users (widely used with Octopus card integration).

**Combined features in one app** — Research showed residents preferred a unified app over multiple facility apps. A single app reduced cognitive load, minimised home-screen clutter, and provided a cohesive brand experience.

### Usability Testing

**Round 1** — Several participants couldn't locate the "Call Lift" feature, which was buried in settings. I moved "Smart Lift" to the main Access tab, and follow-up testing showed everyone could find it.

**Round 2** — Including elderly residents revealed they struggled with a small QR-code scanning area. I enlarged the viewfinder and added real-time visual feedback, which markedly improved task completion for senior users.

## Kiosk Experience

The building experience isn't only on phones. Two kiosk touchpoints needed their own design:

- **Guest Registration Kiosk** — visitors register at a lobby kiosk instead of queueing at the desk. The flow captures identity documents through OCR, collects the visit details, and issues a QR pass — designed to be completed standing up, without staff help
- **Clubhouse Booking Kiosk** — residents browse facilities, check availability, and book on the spot, with payment integrated into the flow

## Pool Locker

Swimmers at the clubhouse pool needed a way to secure their belongings without carrying keys to the poolside. The pool locker flow uses the same QR access as the rest of the building: a resident opens the app, triggers the locker unlock, and the system assigns and opens a locker through the access-control network.

I mapped the functional flow end-to-end before touching screens — from the resident triggering the unlock, through the system locating an available locker, to the unlock confirmation and any failure path:

## Outcomes & Feedback

The unified app replaced multiple physical keys and cards, let residents book the clubhouse and pre-register visitors without phoning management, and gave the building's access a single QR-based system across lobby, clubhouse, mailbox, and locker.

## Reflections

This project reinforced the importance of designing for the moment, not just the feature list. The pool locker flow exists because residents shouldn't carry keys poolside, and the kiosks exist because the front desk shouldn't be a bottleneck — every touchpoint mapped to a real daily ritual rather than a checklist of features. Balancing the needs of tech-savvy professionals who wanted speed with elderly residents who needed simplicity required careful prioritisation and extensive usability testing. Early testing with elderly users is crucial; involving senior residents from the wireframe stage prevented costly late-stage revisions. Offline functionality mattered more than expected — QR code generation needed to work without internet in parking garages with poor signal.
