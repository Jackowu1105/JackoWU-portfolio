## Research & Discovery

I conducted **stakeholder interviews** with property managers, **user interviews** with residents across a range of ages, and **competitor analysis** of smart building apps from similar luxury developments in Hong Kong.

### Key Findings

- Most users wanted opening the app to be faster than using a physical key
- Many had security concerns about unauthorised access if they lost their phone
- Users strongly preferred a clean interface with a handful of core features over many rarely-used functions
- Most residents preferred a single unified app over installing several separate facility apps

## Design Process

### Information Architecture

I organised features into three categories based on frequency of use: **Access Control** (the bulk of daily usage — door, mailbox, lift), **Reservations** (less frequent — clubhouse, facilities), and **Visitor Management** (occasional — kiosk registration).

### Wireframing & Iteration

**V1** — All features on a single scrolling home screen. Users complained about having to scroll to find commonly-used functions.

**V2** — Bottom tab navigation with "Access Control" as the default landing tab, plus quick-access widgets for entrance and lift controls. Task completion became noticeably faster.

**V3** — Accessibility refinement: increased minimum tap targets, added haptic feedback, and implemented VoiceOver support.

### Key Design Decisions

**QR code for locker/access** — Faster than Bluetooth pairing, works offline, and familiar for Hong Kong users (widely used with Octopus card integration).

**Combined features in one app** — Research showed users preferred a unified app over multiple facility apps. A single app reduced cognitive load, minimised home-screen clutter, and provided a cohesive brand experience. The trade-off was a unified app over separate, simpler per-function apps (which would have fragmented the experience and labelled users).

### Usability Testing

**Round 1** — Several participants couldn't locate the "Call Lift" feature, which was buried in settings. I moved "Smart Lift" to the main Access tab, and follow-up testing showed everyone could find it.

**Round 2** — Including elderly residents revealed they struggled with a small QR-code scanning area. I enlarged the viewfinder and added real-time visual feedback, which markedly improved task completion for senior users.

## Visual Design

### Design System

Built a reusable component library across the app screens.

**Color:** A dark, near-black base (`#151819` / `#2D3030`) anchors the smart-building interface with a sense of security and premium feel, layered with deep blue-grey (`#1F282E` / `#364047`) for functional surfaces — aligning with the luxury residential positioning. QR codes and status accents read clearly against the dark theme.

**Typography:** System fonts (SF Pro / Roboto) with a generous minimum body size for readability across age groups.

### Accessibility

WCAG AA compliance (4.5:1 contrast ratios), large tap targets, VoiceOver/TalkBack support, bilingual support (Traditional Chinese / English), and a high-contrast mode for low-vision users.

## Outcomes & Feedback

The unified app replaced multiple physical keys and cards, let residents book the clubhouse and pre-register visitors without phoning management, and cut down routine inquiries to the management office.

## Reflections

This project reinforced the importance of designing for diverse user groups. Balancing the needs of tech-savvy professionals who wanted speed with elderly residents who needed simplicity required careful prioritisation and extensive usability testing. Early testing with elderly users is crucial — initial designs failed accessibility requirements, and involving senior residents from the wireframe stage prevented costly late-stage revisions. Offline functionality mattered more than expected — QR code generation needed to work without internet in parking garages with poor signal.
