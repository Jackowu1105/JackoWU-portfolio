## Problem Definition

The core tension in a robo-advisor app is trust versus simplicity. Investors want to feel in control, but most cannot — and do not want to — parse raw market data. Two failure modes follow:

- **The dashboard trap** — surface every metric, and the app becomes a tool only a professional can use
- **The black-box trap** — hide every decision behind "the AI said so," and users stop trusting it

### Design Goals

- **Plain-language advice as the front door** — the AI assistant is the home, not a feature buried in a menu
- **Make the AI's reasoning legible** — show enough of the "why" that a user can decide whether to follow the advice
- **Let engaged users go deeper without forcing casual users to** — watchlists, notes, and favorites exist for power users, out of the way of the core flow

## Design Process

### Information Architecture

The app is built around a conversational front door and a market-data backbone. I structured it into three primary surfaces, each mapped to a distinct user intent:

1. **AI 助手 (AI Assistant)** — the front door; the conversational advisor. Underneath it, two sub-views — **近期熱門 (Recent Hot Topics)** and **權威指數 (Authority Index)** — surface what the market is paying attention to and a ranked signal layer, so the assistant answers from a base of live context, not in a vacuum.
2. **首頁 (Home)** — the user's own workspace, split into tabs: **推薦 (Recommendations)**, **筆記 (Notes)**, **關注 (Watchlist)**, and **個人 (Profile)**. The recommendation feed is the discovery surface; the others are the user's own collection.
3. **行情 (Market)** — the data layer underneath everything: a market overview, **圖表 (Charts)**, **公司簡介 (Company Profile)**, **基本數據 (Fundamentals)**, **頭條新聞 (Headlines)**, **觀點 (Views)**, and **技術分析 (Technical Analysis)**. This is where the AI's answers are grounded.

The decision to put the AI assistant at the front door — rather than a portfolio dashboard — reflects the product's thesis: the assistant is the product, the data and the dashboard are the reference. **收藏 (Favorites)** and **關注 (Watchlist)** exist as user-collection features inside the Home tabs, not as top-level destinations.

### Authentication & Onboarding Flow

A meaningful share of the design work went into registration, login, and account recovery — the flow that decides whether a fintech user ever sees the product:

- **Registration** — email and phone variants, with verification
- **Login** — combined email & phone entry to reduce "which one did I use?" friction
- **Forgot password** — email and phone recovery paths

### The Notes Feature

筆記 (Notes) is the feature that makes FinSightsAI more than a chatbot. Users annotate positions and research with their own notes — so the AI's recommendations live alongside the user's own reasoning. It is a small surface with an outsized purpose: turn the app from "things the AI tells me" into "things I am thinking about."

## Visual Design

### Color

A restrained, dark-neutral system — warm greys and near-blacks — chosen so that data and the AI's responses are the only things that carry colour. In a fintech app, colour should mean signal, not decoration.

### Architecture

The app is built on a micro-service architecture (advisor, market data, portfolio) so each domain can scale and evolve independently — the advisor can be retrained without touching the portfolio service, and market data can stream without blocking the conversational interface.

## Reflections

Designing a robo-advisor taught me that the hardest part is not the AI — it is the trust layer around it. The assistant has to be confident enough to be useful and transparent enough to be trustworthy, and the IA has to make that legible. Putting the assistant at the front door and the notes feature beside it was the bet that FinSightsAI is a tool for thinking with, not a dashboard to obey.
