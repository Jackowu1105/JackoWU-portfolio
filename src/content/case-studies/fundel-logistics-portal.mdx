## Context — Who FUNDel Is

The product in this case study is FUNDel Limited, a Hong Kong–based fintech company founded and led by Prof. Henry Ko. FUNDel connects e-commerce merchants, logistics companies, and financial institutions to enable data-driven trade and supply-chain finance solutions — with a particular focus on small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) across Greater China and ASEAN.

Two memoranda of understanding frame why this platform exists:

- **19 January 2026** — FUNDel signed an MOU with the Hong Kong SAR Government and the Logistics and Supply Chain MultiTech R&D Centre (LSCM) to leverage trusted logistics data within Hong Kong's Port Community System (PCS) as the foundation for new financial products for SMEs.
- **5 February 2026** — FUNDel signed a further MOU with Tolo Digitech Limited, a spin-off from AIFT (the InnoHK-recognised FinTech research lab cofounded by City University of Hong Kong and Columbia University, with Tsinghua University joining), to develop a trade finance platform on top of PCS logistics data — pairing Tolo Digitech's logistics digitalisation and applied AI with FUNDel's fintech reach, under a strategic equity-exchange arrangement.

The platform I designed and built is the surface where that trusted logistics data turns into something a user can act on — a telemetry-driven quotation, a shipment, a finance facility, a carbon read — in one place. My day job at AIFT put me inside the ecosystem that produced the partnership, and FUNDel is where I got to make the abstract idea of "logistics data becomes finance" into a real, running product.

## Problem Definition

A logistics fintech product has two user populations that never sit in the same room:

- **Operators** — the logistics team running shipments, documents, finance, and carbon reporting
- **Shippers** — the customers who need a quote, a telemetry read, and a way to act on it

Two failure modes follow from building this badly:

- **The split-product trap** — ship two separate apps and the two sides drift out of sync; the operator sees one truth, the shipper another
- **The handoff trap** — a designer draws the whole thing, hands 80 screens to engineering, and waits six months for something that no longer matches the design — or never ships

My version of the brief sidestepped both by keeping it one SPA, and by not handing off at all: I built it myself, with an AI agent as the typing.

### Design Goals

- **One product, two roles** — a single SPA that re-skins by role, sharing one data model and one AI assistant
- **Design fidelity survives the build** — because the designer is the builder, what gets shipped is what was designed
- **An AI-friendly stack** — choose tools that let an AI coding agent produce consistent, reviewable code, not novel-looking code on every prompt
- **Real, not a prototype** — actual data flows (live news stream, 3D globe, document generation), not placeholder screens

## The Vibe-Coding Workflow

This is the part that makes FUNDel different from my other case studies: the build was the work, and the AI agent was the tool.

### The stack, chosen for the AI as much as for the user

I picked the stack partly for how well an AI agent can write in it:

- **React + Vite + TypeScript** — the most-represented combination in the AI's training, so generated code is idiomatic, not exotic
- **shadcn/ui + Tailwind** — component primitives the AI already knows, so it reaches for a known `Dialog` or `Select` instead of inventing a new one each time. This is the single biggest reason the code stayed consistent across a thousand commits
- **TanStack Query + react-hook-form + zod** — standard patterns the agent reproduces reliably, so data fetching and validation look the same everywhere
- **Cursor** as the IDE, with a `.cursor/rules` file enforcing a linear git history — so the AI commits onto the tip of `main` cleanly, no merge-commit soup

The principle: the more conventional the stack, the less the AI improvises, and the more of the design intent survives into code.

### The rhythm: I direct, the AI types

Across ~1,300 commits over five months, roughly **88% were authored by the AI agent** (`gpt-engineer-app[bot]`), ~8% by me directly, and the rest by a few teammates. That ratio is the point — I was not writing every line; I was **directing, reviewing, and integrating**. The workflow on any given feature:

1. I describe the screen or flow in design terms (layout, states, data, edge cases)
2. The agent produces a first pass
3. I review it as a designer — does it match the design? is the hierarchy right? are the states covered? — and request changes in plain language
4. The agent iterates; I merge when it's right

The designer advantage here is concrete: because I can *see* whether a generated UI is good, I reject bad output fast instead of accepting it and patching later. The AI is a fast, prolific junior who never sleeps; I am the senior who keeps the bar.

### Discipline

A few rules kept the AI-generated codebase from rotting:

- **Linear git history only** — a `.cursor/rules` policy forbids merge commits; everything fast-forwards onto `main`. The commit log reads as one clean line, which makes five months of AI-authored history navigable
- **One component library, not many** — the AI reaches for shadcn/ui by default; I do not let it hand-roll parallel component systems
- **Design intent over prompt length** — the shortest path to good output is a clear design instruction, not a longer prompt

## What Got Built

The honest measure of a vibe-coded MVP is not the design — it is what actually runs. Over five months this shipped:

- **16 route-level pages** and **73 feature components** on top of 49 shadcn/ui primitives — ~31,000 lines of TypeScript/TSX in `src/`
- **Operator portal** — Dashboard (with a 3D globe of live shipment routes), Shipments list + detail, Document preview/generation (air waybill, MAWB/HAWB), Customer management, Finance Solutions (loan, KYC), Carbon/Sustainability tracking
- **Shipper portal** — Shipper Dashboard and a telemetry-driven quotation flow
- **AI assistant** — a panel mounted on both portals
- **Real data surfaces** — a live Refinitiv news stream on the dashboard, echarts/echarts-gl for the globe and charts, react-simple-maps for routes, jsPDF + html2canvas for document and invoice generation
- **Auth** — login with email/phone, OTP, reCAPTCHA, forgot-password, sign-up — built to the standard a fintech product is held to, not a demo login

None of this is a clickable prototype. It is a running web app.

## Visual Design

### Color

A data-product palette: a near-black slate base (`#0F172A`), a blue primary for structure and links, an emerald accent for positive/active signal (the AI assistant, target states), and a light surface for daytime and admin-adjacent views. Color means signal — green for on-track, red for exception, blue for structure — not decoration.

### System

shadcn/ui + Tailwind gave a component library that the AI agent could reproduce consistently — the same `Card`, `Dialog`, `Tabs`, `Table` across a thousand commits. The design system survived the build precisely because it was a system the AI already knew.

## Reflections

The lesson of FUNDel is that vibe coding did not turn me into an engineer — it removed the reason I needed to be one to ship. The bottleneck was never typing speed; it was design judgment, and that is the part I already had. The AI agent writes the code; I keep the bar.

A few things I would not have predicted going in. **The stack choice is a prompt-engineering decision**: picking shadcn/ui over bespoke components was not a design preference, it was the single biggest reason the AI's output stayed consistent across a thousand commits. **Reviewing is the job**: ~88% of commits were AI-authored, but every one of them passed my eyes — the designer's ability to *see* bad output is what stops a vibe-coded app from quietly rotting. And **linear git history** turned out to matter more than I expected: a clean commit line made five months of AI-authored history readable, which made the codebase navigable, which made the AI more useful on commit 1,200 than on commit 12.

What I would do differently: involve the discipline of tests earlier — I leaned on visual review, and a test layer would have caught regressions the eye misses. And I would write the design-system rules file (the `.cursor/rules` equivalent for components) on day one, so the AI never had a chance to improvise before the rules existed.
