## Problem Definition

Through user research and testing of the legacy app, I identified the core friction:

- **Complex device setup** — first-time pairing was a common failure point, and users who failed setup often didn't return
- **Slow control response** — delays long enough that users perceived the app as "laggy" or broken, even when technically functional
- **Poor information architecture** — scene and schedule features were buried three levels deep, so users couldn't find them
- **Inconsistent cross-platform experience** — iOS and Android felt like different products

### Design Goals

Rather than chase vanity metrics, I framed the redesign around three honest goals:

- **Halve the effort of device setup** — fewer steps, less room to fail
- **Make the app feel instant** — control should feel responsive, not laggy
- **Lift retention** — the redesign should give users a reason to come back

## Research & Insights

I conducted user research with **MOMAX customers** — roughly **10 user interviews** to understand smart home routines and pain points, plus **usability testing with about 5 users** attempting common tasks on the legacy app. I also reviewed competing smart home apps (Google Home, Apple Home, SmartThings) for interaction patterns, and looked at analytics to find where users dropped off.

Four insights shaped the redesign:

1. **Mental model mismatch** — users think about devices by *location* ("bedroom lights"), but the old app organised by *device type* ("all lights"). That mismatch caused constant cognitive friction.
2. **Speed is critical** — for IoT control, delays over ~2 seconds felt "broken" even when the system worked.
3. **Setup is the make-or-break moment** — users who failed first-time setup rarely returned. First impressions mattered enormously.
4. **Power users want automation, casual users want simplicity** — advanced features (scenes, schedules) had to be reachable but not in the way of first-time users.

### User Persona: "Busy Ben"

A composite persona drawn from the interviews — a 30-something working from home with 8+ MOMAX devices, who wants to control things quickly without thinking and set up new devices in under two minutes. He's frustrated by multi-step processes for simple actions and doesn't intuit the difference between "scenes" and "schedules."

## Design Process

### Information Architecture Redesign

The old app had a flat structure with ~15 menu items, causing decision paralysis. I restructured around the user's mental model into four primary sections:

1. **Home** — quick-access dashboard with favourite devices and one-tap controls
2. **Rooms** — spatial organisation matching how users actually think about their devices
3. **Automation** — scenes and schedules combined, with clearer labelling
4. **Settings** — consolidated account, device management, and preferences

A card-sorting exercise with ~10 users showed strong agreement with the new IA.

### Wireframes & Iteration

I started with paper sketches — about 12 home-screen concepts narrowed to 3 finalists — then built clickable mid-fi prototypes in Figma to test pairing, room management, and automation creation, before developing the full high-fidelity visual design with real device states and motion specs.

### Usability Testing & Iteration

**Round 1 — wireframe testing (~6 users).** Task: add a new smart plug and turn it on. Everyone completed it (a stark contrast with the old app), but users were confused by the "Scenes" label, and several tried to swipe between rooms. I renamed "Scenes" -> "Automation" with clearer icons, and added swipe gestures between room views.

**Round 2 — high-fidelity prototype (~8 users).** Tasks: setup, control, create a schedule. Task success rose sharply versus the old app, the quick-access home widget was a hit, and Android users expected Material Design patterns — so I refined platform-specific UI, added a customisable quick-access widget, and improved visual feedback for state changes.

### Beyond the App

The role stretched wider than app screens:

- **Product UI design** — control interfaces for MOMAX hardware products, from smart plugs to the massage gun companion app
- **App Store preview** — store listing screens and preview graphics that sell the app before download
- **Platform management** — keeping the app platform itself running and updated alongside new hardware launches
- **Video editing** — producing the product demo videos that show each device in action

## Visual Design

### Design System

I built a consistent, scalable component library of ~42 reusable Figma components — device cards (8 variants across device types), control widgets (sliders, toggles, buttons), status indicators, navigation elements, form inputs, and modals/bottom sheets.

**Color:** MOMAX Blue (`#0195E2` / `#02A4DF`) as the primary, paired with semantic states — green for "on", gray for "off", red for alerts, orange for "connecting" — plus an 8-step neutral scale. A deeper blue (`#30465F` / `#1A2F47`) is used for dark device-card backgrounds and the night-time control screen.

**Typography:** SF Pro on iOS and Roboto on Android for a native feel, with a 5-style hierarchy (Hero, H1, H2, Body, Caption).

### Platform Considerations

**iOS** followed Apple HIG — native tab bar/navigation bar, swipe-back and long-press gestures, Dynamic Type and Dark Mode. **Android** followed Material Design 3 — bottom navigation and FABs, Material motion and elevation, adaptive layouts. The two platforms **shared the same IA, task flows, and logic**, with only the visual language adapted to feel native on each.

### Accessibility

- **WCAG AA** — minimum 4.5:1 text contrast
- **Non-color indicators** — device status shown via icon + color, not color alone
- **Touch targets** — minimum 44x44pt (iOS) / 48x48dp (Android)
- **Voice control** — Siri / Google Assistant / Alexa support, with VoiceOver/TalkBack optimisation
- **Large text** up to 200%, haptic feedback for key actions, and a reduced-motion option

## Outcomes & Feedback

The redesign made the app meaningfully faster and simpler to use — device setup that previously took many minutes became a couple of steps, controls felt instant rather than laggy, and features users couldn't find were now one tap away.

## Reflections

**What worked.** Testing lo-fi prototypes early caught navigation problems before they became expensive to fix. Investing in a design system upfront made high-fidelity design and developer handoff smoother — both platforms shared most components. And combining analytics (where users struggled) with interviews (why they struggled) was essential; neither alone was enough.

**Challenges.** Balancing cross-platform consistency with a native feel required deciding when to unify and when to adapt — I landed on shared IA and flows with adapted visual language. IoT latency was partly hardware-limited, so I improved *perceived* performance through optimistic UI updates and clear loading states rather than chasing raw speed alone.

**What I'd do differently.** Involve developers earlier — some design ideas needed complex backend changes that earlier collaboration would have surfaced sooner. And plan for first-time-user onboarding from the start, rather than adding it later as a nice-to-have.
