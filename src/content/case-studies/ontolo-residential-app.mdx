## The Challenge

Great Eagle Holdings wanted a unified resident app, but research showed that **one-size-fits-all interfaces failed to serve diverse resident demographics**.

### User Pain Points

- **Elderly residents (60+):** found typical apps overwhelming with too many options, struggled with small touch targets, felt anxious about making mistakes, and often abandoned tasks due to complex navigation
- **Children (8-12):** needed supervision-friendly features with limited access to sensitive functions (payments, lease info), and preferred visual, icon-based navigation over text
- **Adult residents:** wanted full access to all community services and account management, and expected a modern, feature-rich experience

### Design Goals

- Serve all three demographics with a single app — without patronising the elderly or limiting the adults
- Make core community tasks genuinely usable for the least tech-confident residents
- Keep accessibility as a first-class requirement, not an afterthought

## Research & Discovery

To understand such different user groups, I conducted user research with residents:

- **Contextual inquiries** — observed elderly residents in their homes attempting to use competitor apps. Many abandoned tasks due to accidental taps and navigation confusion, and relied heavily on family members for basic app functions.
- **User interviews with about 20 residents** — spanning ages 8-75, balanced across demographics and digital literacy. Elderly users wanted independence but feared making irreversible mistakes; children preferred visual, icon-based navigation over text.
- **Competitive analysis** of property management and senior-friendly apps. No existing solution offered true adaptive complexity — apps were either over-simplified (patronising) or over-complex (overwhelming).
- **Usability testing of existing solutions with about 5 users** — task success for elderly users was low, with task completion far slower than for adult users.

### Key Insights

**Cognitive load vs. capability** — elderly users aren't "bad with technology"; they're overwhelmed by unnecessary complexity. When I stripped interfaces to core functions in early prototypes, task completion jumped markedly.

**Control & dignity** — users strongly resisted apps marketed as "elderly-friendly" that felt patronising or limited. The ability to switch between complexity levels let users choose based on comfort, not age.

**Pareto principle in features** — across all user groups, a handful of functions accounted for most app usage: emergency contacts, facility booking, community announcements, visitor access, and service requests.

These insights formed the core design principle: **"Simplify by default, expand on demand."**

## Design Process

### Adaptive Interface System

Rather than creating separate apps (which would stigmatise users and double maintenance), I designed a **single app with two adaptive modes** users can switch between:

**Simplified Mode:**
- 5 core functions prominently displayed
- Larger touch targets (60x60pt minimum — well above the 44pt iOS standard)
- WCAG AAA color contrast (7:1) for all interactive elements
- Maximum 2-level navigation depth
- Reduced cognitive load through progressive disclosure

**Full Mode:**
- Complete feature set (15+ functions)
- Standard iOS/Android UI patterns
- Advanced account management
- Multi-step workflows for power users

### Iterations

**Iteration 1 — Concept validation.** Users didn't understand the mode-switching concept during onboarding. I designed an adaptive onboarding flow that suggests the appropriate mode based on the user's stated needs and comfort level. Mode comprehension improved meaningfully.

**Iteration 2 — Information architecture.** Simplified mode still felt cluttered with 8 visible functions. I reduced it to 5 core functions on the home screen and moved secondary features to a clearly labelled "More" section. Task completion time and user confidence both improved.

**Iteration 3 — Mode-switching safety.** Some users accidentally switched modes mid-task, causing confusion. I added a confirmation dialog and moved the mode toggle from the home screen to settings, with a clear explanation. Accidental switches dropped to almost none.

### Key Design Decisions

**Unified app vs. separate apps.** A single app with two modes: prevents stigmatising elderly users, avoids double QA/maintenance, lets families support each other across modes, and keeps a single codebase for faster iteration. Separate apps would fragment the experience and label users of a "senior app."

**Touch target sizing (60x60pt vs. 44pt standard).** Larger targets meaningfully improve accuracy for users with reduced dexterity. I worked with developers on dynamic spacing that prevents button overlap.

**Color & contrast standards.** Simplified mode uses WCAG AAA (7:1 contrast); Full mode uses WCAG AA (4.5:1). I ran ColorOracle simulations and tested with colorblind users — element recognition was faster in simplified mode.

**Navigation depth reduction.** Reduced hierarchy from 3 levels (Full) to a maximum of 2 (Simplified), which cut navigation errors and sped up task completion.

## Visual Design

### Design System

I built a scalable, mode-aware design system with ~42 components that intelligently adapt across both modes — auto-scaling spacing, padding, and interactive states based on mode, on a consistent 8pt grid (with a 16pt unit for simplified mode).

**Typography:** Simplified mode uses 18pt minimum body text (WCAG AAA); Full mode uses 14pt minimum (WCAG AA). Dynamic Type support for user preferences.

**Color:** A calm teal-blue (`#57A3B1` / `#51A1AF`) as the primary, paired with a soft mint-white surface (`#D3E6DF` / `#CEE2DF`) for the simplified mode's soothing background — keeping the screen comfortable and low-anxiety for older users. Full mode uses a clean white base (`#FEFEFE`) with a darker blue-grey (`#42677E`) for text hierarchy.

## Outcomes & Feedback

The dual-mode system served all three demographics from a single app: elderly residents could complete core tasks independently, children got a supervision-friendly view, and adults kept full functionality. Support calls around app usage dropped, and residents genuinely engaged with the community through the app.

## Reflections

This project shaped my core philosophy: **good design isn't about making things beautiful — it's about making things work for real people with real constraints.**

The most elegant solution isn't always the most technically sophisticated. Sometimes it's the one that gives users the power to choose their own path. Designing for elderly users isn't "dumbing down" — it's respecting their intelligence while removing unnecessary friction. Accessibility constraints drove better decisions for everyone: features built for elderly residents (larger targets, clearer labels, reduced complexity) improved usability across all age groups.

**Key takeaway:** Inclusive design doesn't mean simplified design. All users benefit from clarity, but power users still need depth. True accessibility means choice, not reduction.
