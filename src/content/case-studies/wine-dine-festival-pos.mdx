## The Challenge

### Business Problems
- **Long queues** — cash transactions were slow during peak hours
- **Reconciliation burden** — vendors spent significant time counting cash and coupons at close
- **Operational risk** — cash handling in a crowded outdoor environment raised security concerns

### User Pain Points

**Attendees:** carrying multiple payment methods (cash + coupons) was inconvenient, and there was no way to track spending or check balance mid-event.

**Vendors:** mostly part-time food-stall operators with limited tech experience, working under high pressure — noise, crowds, time pressure — where complex interfaces would be unusable. Direct sunlight made low-contrast screens unreadable, ambient noise drowned out audio feedback, and gloves made small tap targets unusable.

**Environment:** wifi at the outdoor venue was unreliable, especially during peak hours — so the system had to keep working without a connection.

### Design Goals
- Make transactions fast enough to clear peak-hour queues
- Make the interface usable for non-tech vendors in under 10 minutes of training
- Keep the system working through wifi outages

## Research & Discovery

There was no formal user-research programme. My understanding of the festival environment and vendor constraints came from **my own observation and reasoning, plus accounts from colleagues** who had worked the event before — what the peak-hour conditions were really like, how vendors operated, and where the old cash-and-coupon model broke down.

From that, three findings shaped the design:

1. **Tech-literacy gap** — many vendors were small-business owners with minimal smartphone experience, so onboarding had to be near-instant.
2. **Environmental challenges** — sunlight killed screen contrast, noise drowned out sound, and gloves ruled out small tap targets.
3. **Offline-first necessity** — wifi at the outdoor venue was unreliable at peak hours, so the system had to function without a connection.

### Design Principles

1. **Glanceable** — all critical info visible at a glance
2. **Tactile** — large tap targets for gloved hands
3. **Resilient** — offline-first architecture with a sync queue
4. **Forgiving** — undo/refund flow for cashier errors

## Design Process

### Information Architecture

I mapped three primary user flows:

- **Token sale** (the bulk of use cases) — RFID tap → amount selection → confirmation → receipt
- **Balance check** — RFID tap → display remaining balance
- **Transaction history** — daily summary for end-of-day reconciliation

### Key Design Decisions

**Single-page transaction interface.** Multi-step wizards demanded too much navigation under pressure. A single dense page traded some visual cleanliness for speed — and most vendors picked it up quickly in training.

**Multi-modal feedback (sound + haptic + visual).** The noisy environment made visual-only feedback insufficient, so every transaction confirmed through three channels at once — meaning a vendor never mistakenly pulled the card too early.

**Offline queue with visual indicator.** Because wifi was unreliable, transactions were stored locally and synced when the connection returned, with a clear indicator showing the sync state.

### Usability Testing

I ran three rounds: lab testing that caught confusion in the refund flow; field testing at a rehearsal event that exposed sunlight readability issues; and a final walkthrough the week before the festival to fine-tune button sizing and contrast.

## Visual Design

### Design System

**Color:** A muted slate base (`#494D5B` / `#787C8F`) with warm tan accents (`#C2A77A` / `#AB8E61`) — high-contrast enough to survive direct sunlight, with a high-visibility green for success states and amber for low-balance warnings. A light-gray background reduces eye strain in sunlight.

**Typography:** SF Pro Display, with a generous minimum body size for outdoor readability and a semibold weight for primary actions.

**Components:** ~12 reusable components (buttons, cards, modals), documented in a style guide for developer handoff.

### Accessibility
- Passed iOS Accessibility Inspector audit
- Tested with ColorOracle for color-blindness simulation
- VoiceOver support for visually impaired staff

### Responsive Design
- Primary: iPad landscape (the devices provided by the Tourism Board)
- Fallback: iPhone mode for backup devices, with the layout staying usable down to a small screen

## Outcomes & Feedback

The cashless RFID system replaced cash-and-coupons across the festival, transactions became fast enough to clear peak-hour queues, and non-tech vendors picked the interface up quickly with minimal training.

The most memorable moment was a real resilience test:

> On Day 2, the primary wifi failed for around 45 minutes during peak hours. The offline queue I'd designed held — it stored 800+ transactions locally and synced them seamlessly once the connection returned. Designing for the worst-case infrastructure paid off.

## Reflections

This project taught me to **design for extreme contexts**. Many UX principles that work in controlled environments — small tap targets, subtle feedback — broke down under real festival conditions. The key was empathising with vendors' constraints: not everyone is a digital native, and that's fine. Good UX meets users where they are.

I'd also do a few things differently next time: simulate the real conditions (heat, noise, crowds) earlier in the process; build a real-time analytics dashboard for organisers; and replace the PDF manual with an in-app interactive tutorial.
